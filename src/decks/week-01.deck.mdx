# What does a sign system need, in order to represent *change* — not just a fixed state?

```notes

```

---

## Naming a state vs. representing change

Most notations are good at **naming** something: a state, a pitch, a value.

Few are built, from the ground up, to represent **a state becoming another
state** — inside the notation itself, not described around it.

That distinction is what today's three cases test.

---

## Case 1 — a traffic light

- **Names:** three states — red, amber, green
- **Where the transition rule lives:** the electronics behind the light

Look only at the sign. You cannot tell what comes next.

```notes
Weakest case: the sequencing is entirely external. A good opener because
it's the most obviously "outside the sign."
```

---

## Case 2 — musical notation

- **Names:** a pitch and a duration, read left to right as time
- **Where the transition rule lives:** mostly an instruction to the performer

Better: a sequence of notes reads as time passing. But a crescendo, a
rubato, a held breath — most of what happens *between* notes is written
beside the notation, not as a state the notation itself transforms through.

---

## Case 3 — binary code

- **Names:** a single two-valued unit
- **Where the transition rule lives:** outside the bit — flipping happens *to* it

A bit is genuinely two-valued. But nothing in "0" or in "1" tells you it is
about to change, or into what.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> All three name a state clearly. None of them notate the transition itself
> — only what came before it, or an instruction for what should happen next.

# This course studies a fourth case

A notation with a specific structural mechanism that marks a state as *about
to transform* — inside the notation, not by outside instruction.
