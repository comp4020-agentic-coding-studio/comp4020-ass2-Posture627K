# Policies and support

These are the rules specific to how this course works, not a general
university policy page — for anything else (extensions on medical or
personal grounds, misconduct processes, disability support), use the
university's own channels; a course page isn't where those live.

## Source use

A factual claim about Taiji, Bagua or the Yijing must trace back to a source
of the same kind the course itself uses: a primary text, an academic
monograph or article, or a museum/archival record — not a tertiary wiki
(Wikipedia, Baidu Baike, and similar) cited as though it settles the
question. Those sites are a fine way to find a source; they are never the
citation itself. This is the same discipline the course's own weekly
[sessions](/sessions/) follow when they name a source inline for a
contested or specific claim.

## Factual claims

When your own work makes a factual claim about this course's subject matter,
say which of the three registers introduced in week 1 it belongs to:
historically attested fact, traditional or legendary attribution, or later
interpretation and modern analogy. Don't present a contested scholarly
position as settled consensus — if you're relying on one scholar's specific
argument (a dating, an attribution), name them. An unmarked claim is treated
as an assertion, not a documented one, and is marked accordingly.

## Assessment work

Assignment 1 and the final project are marked on whether *you* built the
notation and reasoned through it — see [Assessment](/assessments/) for the
weighted criteria. A hexagram traced from a reference diagram, or a
transformation stated without the derivation that produced it, doesn't meet
either brief regardless of whether the final answer happens to be correct.
Show the construction step and the transformation step as work, not just the
result.

## Responsible handling of tradition and interpretation

This course discusses legendary attributions (Fuxi, King Wen, Confucius) and
later reinterpretations (feng shui, Jungian readings, "the I Ching predicted
binary computing") critically, not dismissively. In your own submitted work:

- present a traditional attribution as tradition, not as disproven fantasy
  or as literal history;
- when you critique a modern popular claim, aim the critique at that claim's
  sourcing, not at the broader cultural practice it belongs to.

## Getting help

Ask in session first — most questions about construction, the changing-line
rule, or sourcing a claim are quicker to answer with the builder or the text
in front of both of you. See [People](/people/) for who to ask and how, and
each assessment brief for anything specific to that piece of work.
