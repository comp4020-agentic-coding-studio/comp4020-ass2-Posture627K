## The question

"Taijitu" names more than one image. What actually changed between them, and
what did that change do to how the underlying idea was read?

## What this builds on

Week 2 deliberately withheld any taijitu image while introducing Taiji
itself. This week is where that image finally arrives — as two images, not
one — continuing the second half's pattern of meaning building up in layers
on top of an established structure.

## Two diagrams, two different things to notice

Zhou Dunyi's *Taijitu shuo* (太極圖說, 1017–1073) describes a five-tier
diagram of concentric circles, in order: Wuji (無極, "Non-Polar") → Taiji →
the yin-yang duality → the Five Phases (Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water) →
the myriad things. Read top to bottom, the diagram is an argument: each
tier is a distinct, discrete stage that the one above generates, and the
stages are meant to be told apart.

The familiar swirling black-and-white symbol is a separate, later image,
with documented diffusion from the Ming dynasty onward — not an update to
Zhou Dunyi's diagram, but a different object entirely. Put the two side by
side and the difference isn't cosmetic: a diagram of five discrete,
labelled tiers makes stages and their generative order easy to see and a
continuous whole hard to picture; a single swirling division of black and
white makes continuous, interlocking wholeness the first thing you notice,
at the cost of any of Zhou Dunyi's five stages being visible at all. Two
readers who each know only one of these images would describe "the Taiji
symbol" in almost opposite terms — not because either is wrong about the
picture in front of them, but because a redesigned visual form changes what
a sign makes easy to see and what it makes disappear.

## What you should be able to do after this week

Distinguish Zhou Dunyi's five-tier diagram from the later swirling symbol as
two separate images, name the five tiers in order, and state what the
redesign made visible or invisible rather than describing it as the same
idea in new clothing. Next week follows one specific afterlife this
notation had outside China entirely — and a popular story about it that
overstates what is actually documented.
