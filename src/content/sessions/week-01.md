## The question

Most sign systems are good at naming things: a traffic light names three
states, a musical note names a pitch and a duration. Few notations are built,
from the ground up, to represent *change itself* — a state turning into
another state, inside the notation, not just described around it. This week
sets up that distinction before any course-specific content arrives.

## Reading three sign systems for change, not just state

A traffic light names three states clearly, but the rule that moves it from
one to the next — red follows amber follows green — lives in the electronics
behind the light, not in the light itself. Look only at the sign, and you
cannot tell what comes next.

Musical notation does better: a note names a pitch and a duration, and a
sequence of notes reads left to right as time passing. But most of what
happens between notes — a crescendo, a rubato, a held breath before the next
phrase — is written as an instruction to the performer, not as a state the
notation itself transforms through.

Binary code comes closer again: a bit is a single two-valued unit, and a
sequence of bits can represent anything discrete. But a bit flipping from 0
to 1 is an event that happens *to* the notation, from outside it — nothing
in "0" or "1" alone tells you it is about to change, or into what.

This course studies a fourth case: a notation built from two-valued units
(week 2), combined by a strict rule (weeks 3–4), where a specific structural
mechanism — the changing line (week 8) — marks a state as *about to
transform* inside the notation itself, not by external stage direction. That
is the sense in which this system was built to represent change, and it is
worth holding the other three systems in mind as the comparison case for why
that is unusual.

## What you should be able to do after this week

Compare two or three sign systems (binary code, musical notation, traffic
signals) on how well each one represents transition rather than category, and
hold onto that question as the course introduces a system built specifically
to answer it: yin/yang lines, trigrams, and hexagrams (week 2 begins the
construction).
