## The question

Everything this semester built toward reading one sign system carefully.
Does that reading skill transfer to claims and systems you haven't studied?

## What this builds on

Every earlier week contributed one piece: construction (weeks 1–4),
structural relationships and arrangement (weeks 5–7), transformation (week
8), and sourcing discipline applied to text, image, and cross-cultural
reception (weeks 9–11). This week applies all of it together, on material
you choose.

## Two tests of the same toolkit

The first test looks backward: take a modern popular claim built on this
course's sign system — feng shui's use of the Houtian arrangement,
"quantum yin-yang" analogies, or something comparable — and evaluate it
against primary or academic sourcing, the same standard applied to every
claim since week 1. The skill being tested is not "know more facts about
Taiji and Bagua" — it is the discipline this course has practiced weekly:
telling a historically attested fact from a traditional attribution from a
later interpretation, and being able to say which is which for a claim you
did not encounter in class.

The second test looks outward: a small sign system the course never
covered, analyzed with the same toolkit — what its minimal unit is, how it
combines, whether it has anything resembling arrangement or transformation,
and what would count as attested fact versus received interpretation about
its history. If the toolkit only works on Taiji, Bagua, and the Yijing, it
was never really a toolkit — this is the direct check of whether it
generalizes.

## What you should be able to do after this week

Critically evaluate a modern popular claim against academic sourcing, and
apply the construction/arrangement/transformation/sourcing toolkit to an
unfamiliar sign system as a transfer task. The final project (due this
week) is exactly these two tests — see the [assessment page](/assessments/)
for the brief.
