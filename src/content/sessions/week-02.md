## The question

What is the smallest mark that can carry a distinction — and what happens
when you commit to exactly two possible values for it?

## What this builds on

Week 1 asked what a change-representing sign system needs. This week
introduces the smallest such unit: a single line, broken or unbroken.

## The line, and the sequence it starts

Draw one unbroken line and you have yang (陽); break it in the middle and you
have yin (陰). That is the entire alphabet this notation starts from: one
mark, two states, nothing in between. Committing to exactly two values, no
more, is what makes later stages of the system combinatorial rather than
descriptive — week 3 asks what happens when you stack a second line.

The *Xici* (繫辭, one of the Ten Wings commentaries appended to the Zhouyi)
gives a documented generative sequence for how the rest of the system is
supposed to unfold: Taiji (太極) → Liangyi (兩儀, "the Two Modes" — yin and
yang) → Sixiang (四象, "the Four Images") → Bagua (八卦, the Eight Trigrams).
That sequence is directly textually attested; what Taiji itself names is
less settled. Scholarship renders it variously as "Great Ultimate" or
"Supreme Polarity," and this course does not adopt one fixed gloss — later
weeks meet two very different things called "Taijitu" (a diagram of Taiji)
that are historically separate images (week 10), which is reason enough to
keep Taiji itself as a term in a sequence, not a settled picture yet.

It is tempting, on meeting a two-valued system, to reach immediately for
binary digits as an equivalent. Resist that for now: a structural comparison
(two values, combined by a rule) is fair game and useful; a historical
connection between this notation and modern computing is a specific,
documented, much narrower claim that the course examines directly in week
11 — and the popular version of that claim overstates what is actually
known.

## What you should be able to do after this week

The *Xici* gives a documented generative sequence: Taiji → Liangyi (the two
lines) → Sixiang → Bagua. This week keeps close to that sequence rather than
treating Taiji as one settled cosmological idea — later interpretations
(Zhou Dunyi's diagram in week 10, popular "yin-yang symbol" readings) are
separate objects of study, covered on their own terms later, not folded into
this week's definition. Next week takes the second step in the sequence:
combining two lines.
